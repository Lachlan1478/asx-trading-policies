Headline

  • The loan leg is banned outright in 144 policies for directors and senior executives: 90 ban any secured financing over the shares (or any financing "in respect of" them) and 54 ban margin lending specifically. A further 157 allow it with clearance, 46 with notification or freely, and 106 are silent. Where the rules do reach financing, most (272 policies) reach any secured arrangement, not just margin loans, whether the treatment is a ban or a clearance.
  • Hedging unvested or holding-locked equity is banned wherever it is addressed: 271 policies prohibit it for those shares specifically and a further 87 ban hedging in every state; 12 allow it in some form and 84 are silent. This mirrors section 206J of the Corporations Act. For vested, unrestricted shares held by senior tiers the picture splits: 207 policies permit hedging with clearance, 115 prohibit it, 20 permit it with notification or freely, and 110 do not address it.
  • Collars are rarely regulated by name. The word appears in 57 of the 454 policy texts, usually inside a definition of derivatives. The rest reach the trade through a definition of hedging ("limit the economic risk") or of dealing: across all 6,521 rules, 924 apply only because the dealing definition sweeps the activity in and 367 only through the hedging definition.
  • Enforcement risk is under-drafted. 190 policies expressly carve out a secured lender's enforcement sale, 31 make it a breach by the executive even in a closed period, and 214 say nothing.
  • Clearance is the norm, not a bar. 1,161 of 1,488 clearance regimes are approval-based; the modal validity is 5 business days (498 regimes), then 2, 7, 3 and 10.
  • Substance-over-form clauses appear in 86 policies, which is the main reason a loan without margin-call features cannot be assumed to escape a "margin lending" ban.

The funding leg

Funding leg: is a loan secured on the shares allowed?

Strictest rule on secured financing, margin lending or encumbrance for the senior tiers over vested unrestricted shares. "Banned wide" means the ban covers any secured financing; "banned margin only" means only margin lending is named.

Funding leg: how far the financing rules reach

Which arrangements the policy regulates at all, by ban or by clearance. This is scope, not permission: BHP regulates any secured financing but only requires clearance.

Margin loan only: the rule names margin lending and nothing wider. Any secured financing: any loan secured over the company's shares, whatever the lender or structure; the trigger is a charge over the shares themselves. Any financing in respect of the securities: drafted as "any other secured financing arrangements in respect of [Company] Securities" (Origin, CSL, Transurban). Two things widen it: "in respect of" catches arrangements connected to the shares even where the charge is not over the shares, such as a loan secured over the purchased put; and these policies usually define Securities to include options, derivatives and any financial product over the shares, so a facility secured on the option legs is itself financing in respect of Securities. For a funded collar that is the distinction that matters: under the second bucket a loan secured only over the put is caught on the plain words. The bucket is a reading of the drafting by the extraction, not a bright-line rule, and it records reach, not permission.

Practical patterns for the bank

  • Vesting alone is not enough diligence. Most policies treat vested-but-holding-locked shares like unvested ones. The collared parcel must be vested and free of plan restrictions.
  • Where only "margin lending" is banned (105 policies), structure matters. A term loan with no margin call or loan-to-value trigger sits outside the literal words, but 86 policies apply substance over form. Get written clearance that names the structure.
  • Get the loan approved at inception. Many forced-sale carve-outs (COH, CDA, PRU, YAL, HCW, SMR, IGO among others) apply only where the financing was cleared in advance.
  • Tenor rules can catch the collar itself. BHP treats any instrument with maturity of one year or less as short-term dealing; WTC, ALQ and BSL run 6 to 9 month tests; several policies name forward contracts as short-term dealings.
  • Cash settlement avoids a "change in beneficial ownership" at settlement but does not escape policies whose securities definition includes cash-settled instruments (CBA's does expressly).
  • Expect public disclosure. Appendix 3Y captures a director's collar within five business days, and several policies (SEK, TPG, APE, SMR) disclose loan trigger points to the market.
